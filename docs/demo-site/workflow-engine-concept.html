“Jackets” — an HDPM Workflow Engine

Concept exploration · 7/22/2026 · companion to the Unit Turnover Board plan

1. The idea in one paragraph

Every paper process in the office is the same three things wearing different clothes: a route (the flowchart of who does what, in what order, with what branches), a packet (the form + attached documents traveling that route — today, an orange form in a yellow jacket folder), and a desk (wherever it’s sitting right now, waiting for a human). Build one engine with exactly those three primitives and every form digitizes into it: the Vacancy Tracking Form, Application Processing, the Owner Termination packet we just built, Move-Out Keys, Deposit Accounting. The whiteboard turnover board isn’t a separate product — it’s one view of jackets moving through one route. Working name for an instance: a Jacket, because that’s literally what it replaces. (Manufacturing has the same organism — the traveler packet that rides with a job through every station carrying routing and sign-offs. “Jacket” wins internally because HDPM staff already say it; “traveler” is the candidate outside name if this productizes.)

Two physical behaviors the digital jacket must honor or paper survives alongside it:

  • Every jacket has a back. Paper forms accrete unstructured overflow — work orders handwritten on the back of the Gold sheet, phone numbers, “called 7/18 no answer.” The jacket’s front is the structured tracks; its back is first-class too: linked work orders (live AppFolio records, better than handwriting), freeform notes, attachments — all timestamped, nothing that needs a pen.
  • Color is the route. The office runs a folder color code today (yellow for 30-day/move-out packets; red and green meanings to be confirmed with the team — likely one more color). Digital jackets keep it: folder color = route color on the card border and chip, so a screen of jackets reads the way the shelf does. Action item: confirm the color taxonomy with staff before encoding it.

2. Why the paper version works, and what it costs

The yellow folder is a genuinely good system: it’s visible, it’s unambiguous (one folder, one process, one desk), and picking it up is claiming it. Keep all of that. What it can’t do: be in two places at once (PM’s box vs. front desk), remind anyone of anything, enforce that step 4’s follow-up call actually got calendared, survive being buried under other folders, produce metrics, or be seen from home. The digital version must preserve the pickup-ownership feel — a jacket sits in a queue until someone takes it, and then their name is on it — while fixing the physics.

3. Why not LeadSimple or Realm-X

To be fair to both: LeadSimple is a capable process-automation layer for PM companies, and Realm-X is AppFolio’s own AI/automation surface and will keep improving inside AppFolio. The case for building anyway:

  • Form-native, not checklist-native. Our processes aren’t lists of to-dos; they’re forms that accumulate data as they travel (the Vacancy Tracking Form top section, the fee amounts, the forwarding address). We want the jacket to BE the form.
  • Our ground rules are opinions most tools don’t hold. From the Maintenance OS spec, already proven thinking: every open item has one accountable owner and a next-action date; tripwires fire on silence; a closure gate is server-side validation. A generic tool lets you skip these. Ours makes them physics.
  • Verify-by-read (see §5) — the pattern that makes “integrates with AppFolio but separate from it” actually honest.
  • Realm-X keeps us inside AppFolio’s product decisions; the ops plan’s ground rule is AppFolio stays the system of record but never becomes the workflow brain. LeadSimple is per-seat forever and generic where we want opinionated.
  • We already own the substrate: hdpm-chat has auth, Supabase, chat delivery, AppFolio webhooks + read API, and (per the maintenance-os docs) time/materials, invoicing, and routing modules to pattern-match.

The honest caution: this is how companies accidentally rebuild ServiceNow badly. The defense is §7 — five primitives, no more.

4. Architecture: three layers

Layer 1 — Route definitions (the flowchart). Each process is a declarative definition (YAML/JSON in the repo, versioned like code): stages, who can act (person, role, or pool), form fields collected at each stage, branches, SLAs, tripwires. The flowchart is generated from the definition (Mermaid render) — you read it as a flowchart, you edit it as config. A visual drag-and-drop editor is a v3 luxury, not a prerequisite; resist building it first.

Layer 2 — The engine. A state machine per jacket: current stage, claimed-by, next-action date, field data, attachments, full audit trail. Queue semantics: a stage routes to a person’s box (the primary pattern — routing in the office is intentional, everyone has a named box), a role, or occasionally a pool. SLA timers per stage; silence past the timer = tripwire = chat escalation. Nothing can sit unowned or undated — the Maintenance OS ground rule, enforced at the schema level.

Tracks — the fix for the bouncing folder. Studying the Gold Vacancy Tracking form: it isn’t one checklist, it’s five sub-checklists owned by different desks — TENANT (front desk), OWNER (owner coordination), ADVERTISING, VACANCY (key return), CLOSE OUT (accounting) — and they complete out of order, gated by events, not by each other. OWNER work happens early; VACANCY can’t start until keys physically return; CLOSE OUT comes last. The folder bounces between boxes because paper can only be in one place at a time — the bounce is an artifact of physics, not a feature of the process. So a route is a set of tracks: parallel lanes, each owned by a desk, each activated by its trigger event (notice received → TENANT + OWNER + ADVERTISING tracks open; keys-returned → VACANCY track opens; actual move-out confirmed → CLOSE OUT opens). One jacket, visible in several boxes at once, but every open task still has exactly one owner and one date. The intentional routing is preserved; the bouncing evaporates.

Genuine rework — the other kind of bounce, “this came back to me because something’s wrong” — stays, but as an explicit return-with-reason transition: sender, receiver, reason, timestamp. Which turns bouncing from an invisible annoyance into a measurable one: a jacket’s bounce count becomes a process-health metric, and the routes that bounce most are the ones to redesign first.

Layer 3 — Views, all generated from the definition. No per-process UI code: - Kanban — stages as columns, jackets as cards (this is the “ends up looking like a kanban” instinct, and it’s right — but the kanban is a projection of the flowchart, not the source of truth, which is how you get branches and rules a plain Trello board can’t hold). - My Desk — everything claimed by me + everything in pools I can claim, sorted by next-action date. The replacement for physically walking folders between rooms. - The Jacket view — the form itself, with its documents, history, and a progress strip. What you open when you “pick up the folder.” - Flowchart view — the live Mermaid diagram with a dot showing where this jacket is. Great for training; the SOP and the software stop being separate documents. - Boards like the Turnover Board — a custom projection over jackets of one type. The whiteboard mockup we built is exactly this.

5. The AppFolio seam: automate reads, guide writes, verify everything

The 30-Day Notice process shows the real shape of the work: some steps are pure data entry into AppFolio (enter notice date, set move-out date, uncheck prorate for housing clients). We are not going to robot-click AppFolio, and (per the maintenance-os blocking unknown) write scope is unproven. So three step types:

  1. Auto steps — the engine does it: spawn the jacket from the notice-received webhook, prefill tenant/unit/lease-type from the read API, send the templated Remaining Tenant email, create the calendar hold, post the chat notification.
  2. Guided steps — a human does it in AppFolio, from a checklist item with a deep link to the exact page and the exact values to enter (the screenshots in today’s SOP become the step’s help content).
  3. Verified steps — after a guided step, the engine reads AppFolio back and confirms the notice date, move-out date, and charges actually match what the jacket expects. Green check appears only when reality agrees. This is the killer feature: today, step 5b done wrong is invisible until it bites; here, the jacket simply won’t advance.

That trio is what “separate from AppFolio but integrates with it” means in practice — and it’s a moat neither LeadSimple nor Realm-X occupies: LeadSimple doesn’t verify AppFolio state field-by-field; Realm-X can’t, structurally, treat AppFolio as something to be audited.

6. Worked example: the 30-Day Notice route

Sketch of the actual process as a definition (abbreviated):

route: 30-day-notice
trigger: appfolio.notice_to_vacate.received     # webhook spawns the jacket
branches:
  scope: [all_tenants, one_tenant]              # from notice vs. lease roster
  lease: [month_to_month, fixed_term]
  client: [standard, housing]
stages:
  - intake:            # AUTO — prefill tenant, unit, lease type, dates
      auto: prefill_from_appfolio
      next: vacancy_form
  - vacancy_form:      # POOL: front desk — the orange form's top section
      assign: pool:front_desk
      fields: [forwarding_address?, phone_confirmed]
      tripwire: {no_forwarding_address: schedule_followup(+3d)}
      sla: 1d
  - early_term_fee:    # only if lease == fixed_term
      when: lease == fixed_term
      auto: notify_accounting(fee = 1.5 * rent)
      fields: [fee_amount]
  - appfolio_moveout:  # GUIDED+VERIFIED — enter dates, prorate, 2nd Nature
      assign: role:property_manager
      guide: [enter_notice_date, enter_moveout_date, prorate_unless_housing,
              second_nature_charge, clear_unit_available]
      verify: appfolio.read(tenancy) matches jacket   # won't advance otherwise
      sla: 1d
  - confirmations:     # AUTO — move-out confirmation + auto-pay letter + save to tenant page
      auto: [send_moveout_confirmation, send_autopay_letter, calendar_inspection(+1d_after_keys)]
  - remaining_tenants: # only if scope == one_tenant — requalify at 2.5x rent
      when: scope == one_tenant
      auto: send_remaining_tenant_email(deadline=+5bd)
      assign: role:property_manager
      fields: [paystubs_received, qualifies]
      branch: {qualifies: close, fails: escalate_to_pm}
      sla: 5bd
  - handoff:           # the yellow folder moment — jacket lands in PM's queue
      assign: pool:property_managers   # whoever picks it up
      spawns: turnover-board.cycle     # the Unit Turnover Board takes it from here

One definition captures all three SOP variants (all-tenants / one-tenant / housing client) as branches instead of three near-duplicate documents — and the Mermaid flowchart, the kanban, the My Desk queue, and the audit trail all fall out of it for free.

6b. The Gold form, mapped to tracks

The real Vacancy Tracking form (11/2025 revision) decomposes cleanly — each boxed section is a track with an owner and a trigger:

Track Owner (box) Opens when Contents (from the form)
TENANT Front desk Notice received M/O date saved in AppFolio, confirmation shared, prorated rent, inspection calendared, submitted to PM
OWNER PM / owner coord. Notice received Owner notified + initials, termination fee, new listing rent, W/D removal, utilities/landscaping paid, lease term, availability date
ADVERTISING Leasing Availability date set Utilities/appliance info, AppFolio + website + Craigslist updated, posting attached, ad fee charged
VACANCY Maintenance office Keys returned (the Move-Out Keys SOP) Key return date rules (drop box AM = day before, etc.), house/mail/garage/pool key counts vs. issued, holdover rent Y/N, actual M/O date corrected in AppFolio, keys + file → PM inspection box
CLOSE OUT Accounting Actual move-out confirmed Early termination fee, holdover rent, credits, forwarding address entered, outstanding balances, final accounting, transfer funds, mgmt fees, vendor invoices, mail check to tenant

Two things fall out of the mapping. First, every hand-written rule on the form is expressible as an engine rule (e.g. the key-return date logic in the Move-Out Keys SOP is a pure function of drop-box timing; holdover rent triggers a notify-accounting auto step — the form’s “Email to Penny Charge” line). Second, the Returned Keys SOP is a different species: not a track but a tiny triage route (keys arrive → on check-out sheet? → move-out? → else ask staff/vendors → report to PM → record in AppFolio). Small enough to be the v0 spike’s warm-up: one afternoon, proves the engine on something with a branch and an AppFolio verify step, before the 30-Day Notice route proves the whole track model.

7. Scope discipline (how we don’t rebuild ServiceNow)

Five primitives, hard cap: Jacket, Stage, Assignment (person/role/pool), Field, Rule (SLA/tripwire/branch/verify). No sub-workflows, no parallel gateways, no scripting language inside definitions, no visual editor in v1, no per-customer configurability — this is HDPM’s tool, opinionated on purpose. When a process doesn’t fit five primitives, we simplify the process before we extend the engine (usually the process was the problem). If this ever becomes a product for other PMs — and it might; every PM office has yellow folders — that’s a later fork, decided deliberately, not scope that leaks in now.

8. Sequencing & the Sep 4 decision

  • v0 (spike): engine schema + the 30-Day Notice route, internal only, run in parallel with paper folders for two real notices. Success = staff prefer the queue to the box.
  • v1: + Turnover Board as a projection (the mockup, live), chat digests, verify-by-read on the AppFolio move-out steps.
  • v2: 2–3 more routes (Application Processing, Owner Termination — the packet we just built becomes a route that ends with generating that packet), metrics.
  • Wave-1 maintenance work continues unaffected; this engine is arguably the generalization of the Maintenance OS spec (a work order is a jacket with 8 stages and 12 tripwires). Fold that framing into the Sep 4 build-vs-Jobber-vs-Realm-X decision: the question becomes “do we want one engine under maintenance AND turnovers AND office processes,” which is a different — and stronger — case than build-vs-buy on maintenance alone.

9. Open questions

  • Name the thing: “Jackets” is evocative internally; fine to rename before anyone outside sees it.
  • v0 spike sequencing: Returned Keys as warm-up (an afternoon), then 30-Day Notice with tracks as the real proof?
  • Box mapping: confirm the actual desk/owner for each track (the table in §6b is inferred from the form — validate with the people whose boxes these are).
  • AppFolio write scope (the maintenance-os blocking unknown) — if writes ever land, guided steps can become auto steps one at a time.
  • Definitions live in the repo (versioned, code-reviewed) vs. a database (editable by office admin) — start repo, revisit when routes stabilize.
